\section{Basic Theory}
\subsection{Maxwell's equations}
The Maxwell's equations discribe how eletric feild $\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$ and magnetic field $\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$ behave in given position $\boldsymbol{r}$ and time $t$, including Laguerre-Gaussian beam. In free space, the charge density $\rho$ and current density $\mathbf{J}$ are absent, making the Maxwell's equations four homogeneous differential equations with vacuum permittivity $\epsilon_0$ and vacuum permeability $\mu_0$ and speed of light in vacuum $c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}$. In electrodynamics scheme, the Maxwell's equations are
\begin{align} 
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)&=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}= 0, \label{max1}\\ 
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)&= 0,\label{max2} \\ 
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t) &=  -\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r}, t),\label{max3}\\
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r}, t) &=\mu_0\mathbf{J} + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)= \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t).\label{max4}
\end{align}
\noindent Where $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}$ is the partial derivetives along each of the three coordinate directions. In addition, the four equations can be generalized in potential form which will be derived as follow and will be used in the light-matter interaction theory latter. First, the divergenceless of Eq.~(\ref{max2}) introduce a vector potential $\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$:
\begin{equation} 
\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t).
\end{equation}
\noindent Second, the pressent of $\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$ and the Faraday's law (Eq.~(\ref{max3})) gives a quantity that vanishs with it's curl and gives rise to a scalar potential $\phi(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$:
\begin{equation} 
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=\left(\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)+\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)\right).
\end{equation}
\noindent In terms of  $\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$ and $\phi(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$, then, 
\begin{equation} 
\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi(\boldsymbol{r}, t)-\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)\label{gaugeF}
\end{equation}
\noindent will certainly satisfies both Eq.~(\ref{max2}) and Faraday's law (Eq.~(\ref{max3})).  Finally, the Gauss's law (Eq.~(\ref{max1})) and the Ampère's law with Maxwell's correction (Eq.~(\ref{max4})) can be rewritten as two wave equations for the electromagnetic
potentials
\begin{align} 
&\nabla^2\phi(\boldsymbol{r}, t)+\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)\right)=0, \text{and} \label{max5} \\
&\nabla^2\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)-\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)+\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\phi(\boldsymbol{r}, t)\right)=0. \label{max6}
\end{align}
The two equation remain all the information in Maxwell's equations.

\subsection{Gauge Freedom}
Further simplification of the wave equations (\ref{max5}) and (\ref{max6}) can be achieved by exploiting the gauge freedom of the vector and scalar potentials, 
$\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$ and $\phi(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$. Below, we introduce two commonly used gauges: the \textbf{Coulomb gauge} and the \textbf{Lorenz gauge}. The former is widely adopted in light-matter interaction theory, while the latter is employed here for the derivation of Laguerre-Gaussian beams. 
Superscripts $C$ and $L$ are used in the following derivations to distinguish quantities defined in the Coulomb and Lorenz gauges, respectively.
The condition of the gauges are Coulomb gauge and Lorenz gauge are given as
\begin{align} 
\text{Coulomb gauge condition} : &\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}^C(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=0,  \\
\text{Lorenz gauge conditon} : &\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=-\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\phi^L(\boldsymbol{r}, t).
\end{align}
\noindent Both gauges can simplify the wave equations (\ref{max5}) and (\ref{max6}). In Coulomb gauge, the wave equations turn into  
\begin{align} 
&\nabla^2\phi^C(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=0, \text{and} \label{Cwave1}\\
&\nabla^2\boldsymbol{A}^C(\boldsymbol{r}, t)-\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2}\boldsymbol{A}^C(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial }{\partial t}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\phi^C(\boldsymbol{r}, t).\label{Cwave2}
\end{align}
Where Eq.~(\ref{Cwave1}) has trivial solution $\phi^C(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=0$ and Eq.~(\ref{Cwave2}) can be rewritten as $\nabla^2\boldsymbol{A}^C(\boldsymbol{r}, t)-\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2}\boldsymbol{A}^C(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=0$. In Lorenz gauge, the wave equations (\ref{max5}) and(\ref{max6}) turn into  
\begin{align} 
&\nabla^2\phi^L(\boldsymbol{r}, t)-\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2}\phi^L(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=0, \text{and}\label{Lwave1}\\
&\nabla^2\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r}, t)-\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 }{\partial t^2}\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=0. \label{Lwave2}
\end{align}
The physical quantity $\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$ can be related to vector potentials in different gauges through the relation
\begin{equation} 
\mathbf{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{r})=i\omega\boldsymbol{A}^C(\boldsymbol{r})=i\omega\left(\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r})+\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r})\right)}{q_0^2}\right)
\end{equation}
The derivation of this relation has adopt the ansatz of \textbf{monochromatic wave} for electric field
($\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=\text{Re}\left[\mathbf{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{r}) e^{-i\omega t}\right]$, scalar potentials 
$\phi^L(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=\text{Re}\left[\phi^L(\boldsymbol{r}) e^{-i\omega t}\right]$, vecter potantial
 $\boldsymbol{A}^C(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=\text{Re}\left[\boldsymbol{A}^C(\boldsymbol{r}) e^{-i\omega t}\right]$, and
$\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=\text{Re}\left[\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r}) e^{-i\omega t}\right]$ with $\omega$ angular frequency) and derived from Eq.~(\ref{gaugeF}) under different gauge condition. Where $q_0=\omega/c$ is wavenumber of light 

\subsection{Paraxial Helmholtz equation}
In principle, to obtain the solution of a vector potential we can keep on the derivation with either Eq.(\ref{Cwave2}) or Eq.(\ref{Lwave2}), in conclusion we will take Eq.(\ref{Lwave2}) and leave the reason latter in this section. As the first step, the \textbf{monochromatic wave} ansatz ($\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r}, t)=\text{Re}\left[\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r}) e^{-i\omega t}\right]$ ) turns Eq.(\ref{Lwave2}) into \textbf{vector Helmholtz equation} 
\begin{equation} 
\nabla^2\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r})+q_0^2\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r})=0. \label{VH}
\end{equation}
Next, the \textbf{transverse wave} ansatz ($\boldsymbol{A}^L(\boldsymbol{r})=\hat{\varepsilon}A^L(\boldsymbol{r})$ with polarization vector of unit moduls $\hat{\varepsilon}$) rewritten Eq.(\ref{VH}) as \textbf{scalar Helmholtz equation} 
\begin{equation} 
\nabla^2A^L(\boldsymbol{r})+q_0^2A^L(\boldsymbol{r})=0. \label{SH}
\end{equation}
We further assume that the rays have only small inclinations with respect to the optical axis, a condition referred to as the \textbf{paraxial approximation} in geometric optics. 
In wave optics, this corresponds to a paraxial light beam whose dominant plane-wave components lie close to the beam propagation direction. 
Here, the propagation direction is chosen to be along the $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}$ axis in the angular spectrum. 
As a result, the largest component of a paraxial wavevector $\boldsymbol{k}$ lies along the $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}$ direction, and the vector potential $A^{L}(\boldsymbol{r})$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2 A^L(\boldsymbol{r}) + q_0^2 A^L(\boldsymbol{r}) = 0 .
\label{SH}
\end{equation}
