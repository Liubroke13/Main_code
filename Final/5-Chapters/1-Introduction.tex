\section{Exciton in Transition Metal Dichalcogenide (TMD) Monolayer}

Conventional semiconductors such as diamond, silicon~\cite{roisin2024phonon}, and aluminum arsenide are not suitable for the selective initialization, manipulation, and readout of valley states. This is because they possess multiple inequivalent valleys in their band structures that do not couple distinctly to external optical fields.

The situation changed with the discovery of graphene in 2004~\cite{bostwick2007quasiparticle}, which triggered rapid progress in research on two-dimensional (2D) materials. Graphene has a hexagonal honeycomb lattice that places two inequivalent valleys at the $K$ and $K'$ points of the hexagonal Brillouin zone. Nevertheless, the absence of a bandgap in graphene allows electrons to be thermally excited between valleys, making valley polarization unstable at room temperature.

In contrast, transition-metal dichalcogenide (TMD) monolayers share a similar honeycomb lattice but possess a finite direct bandgap (see Figure.~\ref{fig:MoS2_band_structure}) on the order of $\sim 1$--$2\,\text{eV}$, which lies in the visible to near-infrared spectral range. A TMD monolayer consists of two group-VIA chalcogen atoms, such as sulfur (S) or selenium (Se), with a group-VIB transition-metal atom, such as molybdenum (Mo) or tungsten (W), located between them.

Under optical excitation, electrons in a TMD monolayer can be promoted from the valence band to the conduction band near the $K$ and $K'$ valleys, leaving holes in the valence band. Due to the strong Coulomb interaction in atomically thin materials with weak dielectric screening, the electron–hole pair can bind together to form a quasiparticle known as an exciton. Figure.~\ref{fig:exciton_figure} shows that   center-of-mass (COM) momentum of exciton is related to momentum of light $\boldsymbol{Q}_c$ with incident angle $\theta$ through $\boldsymbol{Q}=\boldsymbol{Q}_c\,\sin{\theta}$, which gives the idea of the light cone. 

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{exciton_figure.pdf}
\caption{Illustration of a photon carrying momentum $\boldsymbol{Q}_c$ exciting (leaving) an electron (hole) with momentum $\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{Q}$ ($-\boldsymbol{k}$), forming an exciton with center-of-mass (COM) momentum $\boldsymbol{Q}$. Schematic of ideal of light cone and relation between COM momentum $\boldsymbol{Q}$ and $\boldsymbol{Q}_c$}
\label{fig:exciton_figure}
\end{figure}

Moreover, the lack of inversion symmetry in the lattice, together with strong spin–orbit coupling from the heavy transition-metal atoms, gives rise to pronounced spin splitting of the electronic bands. At the $K/K'$ valleys, this splitting is typically on the order of a few meV in the conduction band and several hundreds of meV in the valence band, as illustrated in Figure.~\ref{fig:SOI_structure}(a).

The same symmetry considerations also lead to valley-dependent optical selection rules. As shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:SOI_structure}(b), excitons can be classified according to both momentum and spin selection rules. Only excitons with center-of-mass (COM) momentum $\boldsymbol{Q}$ within the light cone, i.e., $|\boldsymbol{Q}| \lesssim Q_c$, and with antiparallel spin configurations can recombine radiatively and are classified as bright excitons (BXs). In contrast, excitons that lie outside the light cone or violate the spin selection rules remain optically inactive and are therefore classified as dark excitons (DXs).

(b) Schematic configurations of excitonic states, including spin-allowed bright exciton (BX), momentum-forbidden dark exciton (DX), and spin-forbidden DX.}

The finer band structure further enriches the classification of quasiparticles. In particular, the lowest bright exciton band, which is initially twofold degenerate, splits into two linearly dispersing branches when electron–hole exchange interactions are included within a pseudospin model. These branches are commonly referred to as valley-mixed excitons~\cite{peng2022tailoring}. 

In W-based TMDs, an additional excitonic state known as the gray exciton (GX) can also emerge. As shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:gray}, GXs emit light polarized perpendicular to the two-dimensional plane (out-of-plane direction). Compared with BXs and DXs, GXs exhibit relatively long lifetimes—typically an order of magnitude longer than BXs (BX $\sim 10$ ps)~\cite{schneider2020direct}—while retaining weak but finite optical activity. These properties make GXs particularly useful for studying exciton dynamics and light–matter interactions, and highlight the rich excitonic physics in two-dimensional TMDs.

\section{Spin and Orbital Angular Momentum (SAM and OAM) of Laguerre–Gaussian (LG) beam}

Following the theoretical work of Poynting in 1909~\cite{poynting1909angular} and the experimental demonstration by Beth in 1936~\cite{Beth1936Mechanical}, it has been established that each photon in circularly polarized light with finite dimensions~\cite{allen1992oam, jauch1976theory, forbes2024orbital} carries a spin angular momentum (SAM) of $S = \sigma\hslash = \pm \hslash$ along the propagation direction, where $\sigma$ denotes the photon helicity. In Beth's experiment, the torque exerted on a suspended half-wave plate by circularly polarized light was measured, demonstrating that spin angular momentum can be transferred to a birefringent medium during propagation (see Figure.~\ref{fig:SAM_OAM}(a)).

In 1992, Allen \textit{et al.} showed that a laser beam in a Laguerre--Gaussian (LG) mode possesses a well-defined orbital angular momentum (OAM), also directed along the propagation axis, with $L=\ell\hslash$ per photon, where $\ell \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the azimuthal mode index, commonly referred to as the topological charge~\cite{allen1992oam}. In their experiment, an input Hermite--Gaussian mode, which carries zero OAM, was converted into an LG mode with nonzero OAM. This mode conversion subsequently induced a measurable torque on cylindrical optical elements (see Figure.~\ref{fig:SAM_OAM}(b, c)), establishing that light can also carry OAM associated with the spatial structure of its wavefront. The helical phase structure associated with a quantized amount of $\ell\hslash$ of OAM is illustrated in Figure.~\ref{fig:OAM_wavefront}, while intensified charge-coupled device (ICCD) images show the characteristic doughnut-shaped intensity distribution.

Since then, a wide range of applications of light carrying OAM has been developed. Early studies focused on the transfer of angular momentum from light to matter, where objects experience a mechanical torque due to the transfer of OAM, resulting in rotational motion~\cite{he1995direct, paterson2001controlled, stilgoe2022controlled}. Later developments have extended to various areas, including optical communications~\cite{gibson2004free, wang2012terabit, bozinovic2013terabit}, quantum key distribution~\cite{vallone2014free, sit2017high}, astronomy~\cite{tamburini2011twisting}, data storage~\cite{ding2013single, nicolas2014quantum}, enhanced sensitivity in imaging~\cite{torner2005digital, uribe2013object}, and optical tweezers~\cite{he1995direct, padgett2011tweezers}. These developments naturally motivate the investigation of how structured light carrying orbital angular momentum interacts with electronic excitations in low-dimensional materials.

\section{SAM and OAM coupling in LG beam}

In free space, coupling between SAM and OAM has been shown to occur in non-paraxial (tightly focused) light. Along the propagation direction, SAM can be partially converted into OAM as the focusing angle increases~\cite{bliokh2015spin}. This effect can be understood by considering the reduction of the spin component along the beam axis induced by a focusing lens (see Figure.~\ref{fig:SAMreduce}).

The initial spin $s_0$ is aligned with the propagation direction. After focusing, however, the spin is no longer parallel to the beam axis; instead, the final spin $s_1$ is tilted, such that only its longitudinal component $s_z$ contributes to the total spin angular momentum of the beam. Since the maximum magnitude of the spin angular momentum is $s\hslash$ per photon, the total spin after focusing must be reduced. However, the total angular momentum is conserved during the focusing process. Consequently, the reduction in SAM must be compensated by the generation of orbital angular momentum, leading to a transfer from SAM to OAM~\cite{nieminen2008angular}.

However, it should be emphasized that the simple proportionality between spin and orbital angular momentum with the optical helicity $\sigma$ and topological charge $\ell$ holds strictly only within the paraxial approximation~\cite{bliokh2015transverse, barnett1994orbital}, which often neglects the longitudinal component along the propagation direction. However, it is precisely this longitudinal component that can couple to GXs. More importantly, this neglected component already contains two distinct types of spin–orbit interaction (SOI).

In 2024, Oscar Javier Gomez Sanchez \textit{et al.} demonstrated that a type of SOI originating from the phase of the longitudinal component induces momentum-dependent transition rates of GXs through the superposition of two twisted-light beams, forming a vector vortex beam (VVB) (see Figure.~\ref{fig:excitation}(a))~\cite{sanchez2024enhanced}. However, the combined contribution of SAM and OAM was not fully clarified in their formulation. Meanwhile, in 2022, Guan-Hao Peng \textit{et al.} investigated the photoexcitation of valley-mixed BXs by LG beams within the pseudospin model, suggesting a connection to excitonic topology~\cite{peng2022tailoring} (see Figure.~\ref{fig:excitation}(b)). Nevertheless, the global phase of the valley-mixed excitons may still influence the excited wave-packet profile.

In this work, we extend the studies of Oscar Javier Gomez Sanchez \textit{et al.} and Guan-Hao Peng \textit{et al.} to investigate the interplay between spin and orbital angular momentum of light in determining transition rates and wave-packet distributions of excitonic states within SI units.
